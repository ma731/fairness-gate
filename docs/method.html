Method

How it was measured, and what I refused to measure.

Split by time and by geography, three fairness criteria reported at once because they contradict each other, a floor under what gets published, and the experiment that tests the fix everybody suggests first.

Method

Four decisions that shaped every number above.

The split is temporal, not random

Fitted on 2015, tuned on 2016, tested on 2018, which is how a model like this is actually used: built on the past, applied to the present. A random split of a single year would have hidden the drift entirely.

Four states are held out completely

Geographic shift on top of temporal shift. The positive rate falls to 0.302 there against 0.386 in the test states, and AUC falls from 0.888 to 0.864. That decay is measured rather than assumed away.

Protected attributes are kept out of the features

Race and sex are held separately and used only to measure disparity, never to predict. An attribute is protected whether or not the model is allowed to see it, so the audit runs either way.

Three fairness criteria are reported together

Demographic parity, equalised odds and calibration cannot all hold when base rates differ across groups, and here they differ by 0.270. Quoting one number alone would be a choice about which unfairness to make invisible.

The obvious fix

What happens if the model cannot see race or sex.

This is the first thing almost everybody suggests, including me before I measured it. It has a name, fairness through unawareness: if the model cannot see the attribute, it cannot discriminate on it. It is testable, so I tested it instead of citing someone.

The model does use race and sex. So I trained it again with both columns deleted, gave it its own threshold, and scored it on the same year.

The lines barely move. The model has no idea what race anyone is and it still finds about 54 out of every hundred qualifying people in one group and 85 in another. It rebuilds nearly all of the signal from occupation, education, hours worked and place of birth, because those carry the history of who got which opportunities.

Deleting the column does not delete the problem. It deletes your ability to see it, while leaving you feeling like you did something.

A second model

Would a different algorithm simply not have this problem?

Every other number here comes from one gradient boosted tree. The fair objection is that this might be a fact about the algorithm: boosting is very good at finding interactions, and occupation crossed with region and hours could rebuild race without ever being told it.

So I trained a logistic regression on the identical splits and features. Linear, additive, no interactions unless you build them.

The linear model is a slightly worse predictor and has a slightly larger gap. Swapping out the entire algorithm moved the disparity by 0.0035. And the rank correlation says the two families do not merely fail equally hard, they fail the same people: rank the groups by recall under each model and you get almost the same order.

Two model families with nothing in common reach the same disparity and put it on the same groups. So the problem is in the data, not the algorithm, and picking a different library is not a plan.

Sources

Where the data and the definitions come from.

Nothing on this page is invented. The people are real survey respondents, the fairness criteria are the standard ones, and the reason they cannot all be satisfied at once is a proved result, not an opinion.

  • American Community Survey, Public Use Microdata US Census Bureau. The survey responses behind every number here, published de-identified for exactly this kind of analysis.
    2015–2018
  • Retiring Adult: New Datasets for Fair Machine Learning Ding, Hardt, Miller and Schmidt. The paper behind folktables, which defines this prediction task and argues for exactly the temporal and geographic splits used here.
    2021
  • Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning Hardt, Price and Srebro. Defines equalised odds, the criterion the true positive gap on this page is measured against.
    2016
  • Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan. Proves that calibration and equal error rates cannot hold together when base rates differ, which is why this page reports three criteria instead of choosing one.
    2016
  • Fair Prediction with Disparate Impact Chouldechova. The same impossibility reached independently, in the context of recidivism scoring.
    2017
  • Fairness and Machine Learning Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan. The standard textbook, free online, and the source for treating a protected attribute as something you audit against rather than something you delete.
    2023
  • EU Artificial Intelligence Act Regulation 2024/1689. Annex IV sets the structure of the technical documentation this project generates alongside the page.
    2024
